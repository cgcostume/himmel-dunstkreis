starting WebGPU…

A physically based sky, computed on the GPU. Sunlight scattered by air, aerosols and ozone, for any time, any place on Earth and any observer altitude within the atmosphere. A WebGPU library in TypeScript, ported from osgHimmel, built to drop into a renderer you already have rather than to be one.

The sky above is rendered live in your browser by the library itself: drag to look around, and every control below takes effect immediately. It is not an image or a gradient, but the result of integrating scattering through an atmosphere, which is why moving the Sun below the horizon gives you a real sunset and a real blue hour rather than a fade to black. The lookup tables that make it fast are shown further down and can be downloaded as PNGs.

Time and place

The Sun's position is computed by @himmel/sternzeit, a separate library, and handed to this one as a plain direction vector. The two share no code and neither depends on the other: whoever embeds this can compute that vector however they like.

Observer

warp view rays

Height is above sea level, capped at the top of the atmosphere: this library assumes an observer inside it, so views from space are out of scope. Refraction is applied per view ray rather than as a single correction to the Sun's direction, which is what bends the low Sun into an ellipse and keeps it visible after it has geometrically set. It is a compile-time switch, so turning it off leaves no refraction code in the pipeline at all.

Atmosphere

Changing any of these re-runs the precompute, which is why the fast variant is worth having: the tables below are rebuilt in a millisecond or so. The osgHimmel preset restores the original's values, a much thicker and less forward-scattering aerosol layer and no ozone at all.

Precomputed tables

These are what the library actually produces. All three are computed by WebGPU compute shaders writing into storage textures, and the sky above is little more than one lookup into the third per pixel. They are shown tone mapped so the values are visible; the real ones are rgba16float.

transmittance

How much light survives the trip from a point in the atmosphere to space. Horizontal axis is the view angle, from straight up on the left to along the horizon on the right; vertical is altitude. Red survives the long grazing paths where blue has been scattered away entirely, which is the whole reason sunsets are red.

multiple scattering

Light that bounced more than once before reaching the eye, every order summed at once. Horizontal axis is the Sun's elevation, vertical is altitude. This is what keeps twilight blue instead of grey, and what a single-scattering model leaves out.

sky view

The sky itself, as a table: every view direction for the current Sun and observer. Rebuilt each time anything moves, which is what makes the render pass a single texture fetch. The horizon sits halfway up, with most of the resolution spent around it.

Part of himmel, a family of small, independent, composable libraries for rendering the sky, each usable on its own:

  • sternzeit, astronomical position math
  • dunstkreis, WebGPU atmospheric scattering (this one)
  • gewoelk, clouds planned
  • sternenzelt, star rendering planned
  • nachtgestirn, moon rendering planned
